Unit 1  •  Blood Structure & Function  LO1

1.1 Erythrocytes (RBCs) and Oxygen Delivery

RBCs are biconcave disks packed with hemoglobin (Hgb), a protein with four globin chains and four heme (iron-containing) groups. Each Hgb molecule carries up to 4 O₂ molecules. Lifespan: ~120 days. Production happens in red bone marrow and is driven by erythropoietin (EPO) from the kidney.
↓ Tissue O₂ → Kidney senses hypoxia → EPO release → Bone marrow produces more RBCs → ↑ O₂-carrying capacity → Tissue oxygenation restored

Why oxygen delivery is the bottom line. The whole reason to have RBCs is to carry oxygen to tissues. O₂ delivery = cardiac output × arterial O₂ content. Any fall in Hgb, SaO₂, or cardiac output reduces delivery. The body responds with both long-term increases in RBC production (EPO) and short-term increases in heart rate and respiratory rate to compensate.

"Which leukocyte function most directly eliminates invading microorganisms?" Phagocytosis — neutrophils and macrophages literally engulf and digest pathogens. Antibody production is more indirect (it tags pathogens for phagocytosis or complement-mediated killing).

1.3 Platelets and Primary Hemostasis

Platelets are anucleate cell fragments shed from megakaryocytes in the marrow. Lifespan ~8–10 days. Normal count: 150,000–400,000/μL. When a vessel is injured, platelets adhere (via von Willebrand factor) → activate → aggregate → form a temporary platelet plug (primary hemostasis). The clotting cascade then deposits fibrin (secondary hemostasis) to stabilize the clot.
Vessel injury → vasoconstriction → platelet adhesion + activation + aggregation → platelet plug → coagulation cascade → fibrin mesh stabilizes clot

Practice Questions — Blood Structure & Function

Q1. Which leukocyte function is most directly responsible for eliminating invading microorganisms?
A. Antibody production by plasma cells
B. Phagocytosis by neutrophils and macrophages
C. Histamine release by basophils
D. Cytokine secretion by helper T cells
Show answer
B. Phagocytes (neutrophils first, macrophages in tissue) directly engulf and digest pathogens. Antibodies tag, but the killing is largely done by phagocytes and complement.
Q2. Which finding would the nurse most expect in a patient with thrombocytopenia?
A. Hemarthroses (deep joint bleeds)
B. Petechiae and easy bruising
C. Pallor and fatigue only
D. Recurrent bacterial pneumonia
Show answer
B. Platelet deficiency produces mucocutaneous bleeding (petechiae, ecchymoses, mucosal bleeding). Hemarthroses suggest a coagulation factor problem (e.g., hemophilia).
Q3. After blood donation, which mechanism most directly restores red blood cell count over the following weeks?
A. Splenic release of stored RBCs
B. Erythropoietin from the kidneys stimulates marrow erythropoiesis
C. Hepatic synthesis of new hemoglobin in the liver
D. Lymphocyte proliferation
Show answer
B. Reduced O₂-carrying capacity is sensed by the kidneys, which release EPO → bone marrow ramps up RBC production → reticulocyte count rises → Hgb returns toward baseline over weeks.

Unit 2  •  Anemia  LO2

Anemia = decreased Hgb / RBCs / Hct → reduced O₂-carrying capacity → tissue hypoxia. The body compensates with several mechanisms; only severe or rapidly developing anemia overwhelms compensation.

"Microcytic = small Iron / chronic disease. Macrocytic = big B12 / Folate."
Reading the MCV is your first move on any anemia question — it splits the differential immediately.
Hemolytic anemia signature. When RBCs are destroyed, the body sees: anemia + jaundice (from heme breakdown to bilirubin) + elevated indirect bilirubin + elevated LDH (released from lysed cells) + low haptoglobin (binds free Hgb). The reticulocyte count is typically high because the marrow tries to keep up.
Why anemia of chronic disease has paradoxically adequate iron stores. Inflammatory cytokines (IL-6) raise hepcidin, a hormone that locks iron inside macrophages and blocks intestinal absorption. Total body iron is fine — it's just unavailable to the marrow. Iron supplementation alone won't fix it; treating the underlying disease will.

2.2 Compensatory Responses to Anemia

Cardiovascular. ↑ heart rate, ↑ stroke volume, ↑ cardiac output → push more blood per minute through tissues.
Respiratory. ↑ respiratory rate and depth → maximize SaO₂ and O₂ uptake.
Marrow. ↑ EPO from kidneys → ↑ erythropoiesis → ↑ reticulocyte count.
At the tissue level, oxygen unloading from Hgb increases (right shift of the O₂-Hgb dissociation curve) thanks to ↑ 2,3-BPG, ↑ CO₂, ↓ pH. This means each Hgb molecule gives up more of its O₂ to tissues that need it most.
"Which mechanisms directly improve oxygen availability at the tissue level?" Answers include: increased cardiac output, increased respiratory rate, increased EPO-driven erythropoiesis, and right shift of the O₂-Hgb dissociation curve (more O₂ released to tissues).

Practice Questions — Anemia

Q4. A patient has microcytic, hypochromic erythrocytes. Most likely cause?
A. Vitamin B12 deficiency
B. Folate deficiency
C. Iron deficiency
D. Acute blood loss
Show answer
C. Microcytic + hypochromic = iron deficiency. Without iron, heme synthesis fails and RBCs are smaller and paler.

A. Loss of intrinsic factor
B. Hepcidin elevation locking iron in macrophages and blocking absorption
C. Bone marrow infiltration
D. Hemolysis from antibody binding
Show answer
B. Anemia of chronic disease — IL-6 → hepcidin → iron stays sequestered. Total body iron is normal/high but functionally unavailable. Treating inflammation is the answer.
Q6. A patient presents with anemia, jaundice, and elevated indirect bilirubin. Which type of anemia is most likely?
A. Iron deficiency
B. Hemolytic anemia
C. Folate deficiency
D. Acute blood loss
Show answer
B. Hemolysis releases heme → broken down to bilirubin → unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin rises → jaundice. ↑ LDH and ↑ reticulocytes complete the picture.
Q7. A patient is diagnosed with vitamin B12 deficiency anemia. Which finding is most expected?
A. Microcytic, hypochromic RBCs
B. Macrocytic RBCs and possible neurologic symptoms (paresthesia, ataxia)
C. ↑ indirect bilirubin and jaundice
D. Pancytopenia
Show answer
B. B12 deficiency → impaired DNA synthesis → macrocytic RBCs. B12 is also required for myelin synthesis → neurologic symptoms differentiate it from folate deficiency.
Q8. Select all that apply. Which mechanisms directly improve oxygen availability at the tissue level in anemia?
A. Increased cardiac output
B. Increased respiratory rate and depth
C. Increased erythropoietin → erythropoiesis
D. Right shift of the O₂-Hgb dissociation curve
E. Reduced kidney function
Show answer
A, B, C, D. All four are physiologic compensations for hypoxia. E is the opposite — kidney function (and EPO) declining would worsen anemia.

B. Same axis as in altitude adaptation — kidneys sense hypoxia and produce EPO, which drives marrow to make more RBCs. Hct can rise enough to increase blood viscosity and thrombosis risk.

Unit 3  •  Bleeding & Clotting Disorders  LO2

3.1 Thrombocytopenia

Thrombocytopenia = platelet count < 150,000/μL. Bleeding risk rises as count falls. Spontaneous bleeding is rare above 50,000; serious risk < 20,000; life-threatening < 10,000.

Mechanisms:

  • Decreased production — marrow failure (aplastic anemia, leukemia, chemotherapy, radiation, B12 deficiency)
  • Increased destruction — immune (ITP), drug-induced, DIC, TTP/HUS, hypersplenism
  • Sequestration — splenic enlargement traps platelets

Classic presentation: petechiae, ecchymoses, mucosal bleeding (gums, nose), prolonged bleeding from minor cuts. Heavy menstruation. No deep bleeds (those suggest coagulation factor problems).

3.2 Polycythemia and Thrombosis

Polycythemia = elevated RBC mass (Hct typically > 50%). Causes:
  • Primary (polycythemia vera) — myeloproliferative disorder; bone marrow makes too many RBCs autonomously
  • Secondary appropriate — chronic hypoxia (high altitude, COPD, smoking, sleep apnea, congenital heart disease) → ↑ EPO → ↑ RBC
  • Secondary inappropriate — EPO-secreting tumor (renal cell carcinoma)
Why polycythemia raises thrombosis risk. Increased Hct = increased blood viscosity. Slower flow + more cells touching each other = greater platelet aggregation and clot formation. Patients can develop strokes, MIs, DVT, PE. Treatment includes phlebotomy and management of the underlying cause.

3.3 Thrombosis — Virchow's Triad

Virchow's triad — three drivers of thrombosis:
  • Endothelial injury — atherosclerosis, surgery, trauma, central line
  • Stasis (slow flow) — immobility, atrial fibrillation, varicose veins, prolonged sitting
  • Hypercoagulability — inherited (Factor V Leiden, antithrombin deficiency), acquired (cancer, pregnancy, oral contraceptives, polycythemia)
Immobility-related thrombosis hits all three Virchow drivers in a hospitalized or post-surgical patient: stasis from lying still, endothelial injury from surgery, hypercoagulable state from inflammation. Prophylactic anticoagulation, sequential compression devices, and early ambulation are all preventive.

Practice Questions — Bleeding & Clotting

Q10. A patient with thrombocytopenia develops prolonged bleeding after minor injury. Which mechanism best explains the prolonged bleeding?
A. Coagulation factor deficiency
B. Inadequate platelet plug formation due to low platelet count
C. Increased fibrinolysis
D. Endothelial cell dysfunction
Show answer
B. Platelets are the first responders to vessel injury, forming the primary hemostatic plug. With too few platelets, the plug doesn't form effectively and minor injuries bleed longer than expected.
Q11. A patient with polycythemia develops increased risk of thrombosis. The best explanation is:
A. Excess platelet count alone
B. Increased blood viscosity from elevated RBC mass slows flow and promotes clot formation
C. Hypoxia-induced vasodilation
D. Iron deficiency
Show answer
B. More RBCs = thicker blood = slower flow + more platelet aggregation. Strokes, MIs, and DVTs are all more common in untreated polycythemia.
Q12. A patient who has been immobile for several days develops thrombosis. Which Virchow's triad component most directly explains this?
A. Endothelial injury alone
B. Venous stasis from prolonged immobility
C. Decreased hepatic clotting factor synthesis
D. Decreased platelet count
Show answer
B. Stasis is the dominant driver in immobile patients (though inflammation/hypercoagulability also play in). Early mobilization, compression devices, and prophylactic anticoagulation prevent this.

Unit 4  •  White Cell Disorders  LO2

4.1 Leukopenia and Neutropenia

Leukopenia = WBC count < 4,000/μL. Neutropenia = absolute neutrophil count (ANC) < 1,500. Severe neutropenia (ANC < 500) is a medical emergency for infection risk.

Common causes:

  • Chemotherapy — most common cause; nadir typically 7–14 days after dose
  • Radiation
  • Bone marrow infiltration (leukemia, lymphoma, metastases)
  • Aplastic anemia
  • Severe infections (paradoxically — sepsis can deplete neutrophils)
  • Autoimmune neutropenia
  • Drug-induced (clozapine, methimazole, others)
  • B12 / folate deficiency
The neutropenic patient can have profound infection without classic signs because they cannot mount inflammation. Fever may be the only sign. Neutropenic fever requires immediate broad-spectrum IV antibiotics — even before culture results.

Practice Questions — White Cell Disorders

C. Reduced erythropoietin production

Show answer
B. Without enough neutrophils and other WBCs, the immune system can't clear bacteria, fungi, and other pathogens efficiently. Neutropenic fever requires immediate empirical antibiotics.

B. Chemo doesn't discriminate well — it hits all rapidly dividing cells, including bone marrow precursors. Result: predictable cytopenias 7–14 days after dosing. Neutrophil nadir is the highest infection-risk window.

B. Even with high "WBC counts" on labs, leukemic blasts can't fight infection, and the marrow can't make functional cells.

Unit 5  •  Pediatric Hematology  LO3

5.1 Sickle Cell Disease

Autosomal recessive mutation in the β-globin gene → abnormal hemoglobin S (HbS). Under low O₂, acidosis, dehydration, or stress, HbS polymerizes, deforming RBCs into rigid, sickle-shaped cells. These cells:
  • Block small vessels → vaso-occlusive crises → pain, organ ischemia
  • Are destroyed faster than normal → chronic hemolytic anemia
  • Damage the spleen by repeated infarctions → functional asplenia → susceptibility to encapsulated bacteria (S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, Salmonella)

Classic features:

  • Recurrent severe pain crises (back, chest, extremities, abdomen)
  • Acute chest syndrome — sickling in pulmonary vessels (medical emergency)
  • Splenic sequestration (acute drop in Hgb in young children)
  • Stroke (pediatric stroke risk significantly elevated)
  • Dactylitis (hand-foot swelling from infarction in small bones)
  • Chronic hemolytic anemia with elevated reticulocyte count
  • Increased gallstones (chronic bilirubin overload)
Triggers of vaso-occlusive crisis: dehydration, hypoxia, acidosis, infection, cold exposure, stress. Treat acutely with hydration, oxygen, pain control. Prevent with hydroxyurea (raises HbF, which doesn't sickle).

5.2 Neonatal Jaundice and Hemolytic Disease of the Newborn

Physiologic jaundice appears after 24 hours of life and resolves within a week. It reflects the immature liver's limited ability to conjugate the bilirubin produced as fetal hemoglobin is broken down.
Pathologic jaundice appears within 24 hours of birth and signals abnormal hemolysis or other pathology. Unconjugated bilirubin can cross the BBB and cause kernicterus — irreversible neurologic damage.
Hemolytic disease of the newborn (HDN). An Rh-negative mother previously sensitized to Rh-positive fetal blood (often during a prior pregnancy or delivery) makes IgG anti-Rh antibodies. In a subsequent Rh-positive pregnancy, these antibodies cross the placenta and destroy fetal RBCs → fetal anemia, jaundice, hydrops fetalis. RhoGAM (anti-D Ig given at 28 weeks and within 72 h of delivery) prevents maternal sensitization.
Why a newborn with jaundice within 24 hours is a red flag. Physiologic jaundice can't appear that fast — it requires the neonatal liver to gradually fall behind. Within-24-hour jaundice means active hemolysis (HDN, ABO incompatibility, G6PD deficiency, sepsis). Don't wait.

Premature infant anemia

Premature infants commonly develop anemia during their first weeks of life. Causes: shortened RBC lifespan, frequent blood draws (a substantial portion of small infants' blood volume), low EPO production by the immature kidney, rapid growth diluting Hgb. Often requires transfusion or supplemental EPO.

B. Vaso-occlusive crisis. Sickled cells are rigid and block microvasculature → ischemic pain. Triggers include dehydration, hypoxia, infection, cold, and stress. Hydration, O₂, pain control are mainstays.

B. Within 24 hours = pathologic. Active hemolysis is the most common cause; check Rh and ABO status, hemolysis labs. Bilirubin crossing the BBB causes kernicterus.
Q18. A newborn with hemolytic disease of the newborn develops anemia. The condition is best prevented by:
A. Routine vitamin K injection

C. Folic acid in the third trimester
D. Iron supplementation in pregnancy
Show answer
B. RhoGAM binds and removes any fetal Rh-positive RBCs that have entered maternal circulation, preventing the mother from making lasting anti-Rh antibodies.

B. The body increases cardiac output and respiratory rate to compensate for reduced oxygen delivery
C. Iron toxicity
D. Vitamin overdose
Show answer
B. Anemia → reduced O₂ delivery → reflex sympathetic activation → ↑ HR + ↑ RR + ↑ stroke volume to maintain tissue oxygenation.
Q20. A premature infant develops anemia during the first weeks of life. Which factor most directly contributes?
A. Excessive iron intake
B. Low neonatal EPO production by the immature kidney plus shortened RBC lifespan and frequent blood draws
C. Increased erythropoiesis
D. Early gut microbiome
Show answer
B. "Anemia of prematurity" — multifactorial: low EPO, fragile RBCs, blood loss from sampling, rapid growth diluting Hgb. May need transfusion or recombinant EPO.

A. Acidosis from kidney failure
B. Compensatory increase in ventilation to maximize oxygen uptake and delivery
C. Hyperventilation from anxiety only
D. Pulmonary embolism
Show answer
B. Chronic anemia → low tissue O₂ → chemoreceptor and central drive to breathe more, raising SaO₂ and total O₂ uptake.

B. The marrow is responding appropriately by ramping up RBC production

B. Reticulocytes are immature RBCs newly released from marrow. A high count means the marrow recognizes the anemia and is responding. A low count in anemia means the marrow is failing.

Unit 6  •  Environmental & Systemic Effects  LO4

6.1 High Altitude — Adaptive Polycythemia

At high altitude, atmospheric O₂ partial pressure is lower → lower SaO₂ → tissue hypoxia. The body adapts:
  • Acute: hyperventilation increases SaO₂ partially
  • Days to weeks: kidney releases EPO → bone marrow produces more RBCs → Hct rises
  • Right shift of O₂-Hgb dissociation curve (↑ 2,3-BPG) → more O₂ unloaded to tissues
This is "secondary appropriate" polycythemia. It improves O₂ delivery but raises blood viscosity and thrombosis risk.

6.2 Radiation Exposure

Ionizing radiation damages rapidly dividing bone marrow precursors. Severe exposure → aplastic anemia / pancytopenia: low RBCs (anemia), low platelets (bleeding), low WBCs (infection). The marrow is hypocellular on biopsy. Treatment: supportive care, transfusions, growth factors, possible stem-cell transplant.

6.3 Environmental Toxins

Several environmental exposures damage the marrow or RBCs directly:

  • Benzene — aplastic anemia, leukemia (refining, gasoline, plastics)
  • Lead — microcytic anemia (interferes with heme synthesis), basophilic stippling, neurotoxicity in children
  • Radiation — pancytopenia, leukemia
  • Pesticides, solvents — variable marrow effects

6.4 Viral Infection and Anemia

Some viral infections trigger systemic inflammation that decreases hemoglobin via the same hepcidin-mediated mechanism as anemia of chronic disease. Inflammatory cytokines suppress erythropoiesis and limit iron availability. The anemia is typically mild, normocytic, and resolves with the infection. Severe viral illness (parvovirus B19) can directly suppress RBC precursors, causing transient aplastic crisis — particularly dangerous in patients with chronic hemolysis like sickle cell disease.

6.5 Post-Donation Recovery

After whole-blood donation (~500 mL):

  • Plasma volume restores in 24–48 h via fluid intake and shift from interstitium
  • RBC mass recovers over 4–8 weeks via EPO-driven marrow erythropoiesis
  • Iron stores can take longer; iron supplementation often recommended for frequent donors

Week 8 Study Strategy

  • Read MCV first on every anemia question. It splits the differential before you read another lab.
  • Pair every anemia type with its mechanism. Iron = no heme. B12 = no DNA + no myelin. ACD = hepcidin lockup. Hemolytic = destruction. Aplastic = marrow gone.
  • Memorize the compensations cold. ↑ HR, ↑ RR, ↑ EPO, right shift O₂-Hgb curve. SATA classics.
  • Don't mix bleeding patterns. Mucocutaneous = platelet. Deep / hemarthroses = factor.
  • "Within 24 hours" jaundice in newborns is pathologic. Don't pick the "physiologic" distractor.
  • Sickle cell triggers are extremely high yield — dehydration, hypoxia, acidosis, infection, cold, stress.
  • Connect environmental exposures to mechanisms. Altitude = EPO. Radiation = pancytopenia. Lead = microcytic. Benzene = aplastic / leukemia.
